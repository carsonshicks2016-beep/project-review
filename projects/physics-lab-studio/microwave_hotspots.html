1. The Standing Wave Trap

Microwaves inside the metal box bounce back and forth. Because the waves are confined, they form a Standing Wave pattern. Certain spots (antinodes) have oscillating electric fields that swing wildly up and down, while other spots (nodes) remain perfectly still with ZERO electric field. If you don't use a turntable, food in the nodes stays completely cold!

2. Dielectric Heating

Why do microwaves heat food but not glass? It's Dielectric Heating. Water molecules are polar (they have positive and negative ends). The intense electric field in the microwave flips direction 2.45 billion times per second (2.45 GHz). The water molecules try to flip along with the field, rubbing against each other. This molecular friction creates immense heat!

3. Measure Speed of Light

The distance between two adjacent hot spots (nodes to nodes or antinodes to antinodes) is exactly half a wavelength (λ/2). By melting a chocolate bar without the turntable, measuring the distance d between melted blobs, and knowing your microwave's frequency f (usually 2.45 GHz), you can calculate the speed of light:
c = 2 × d × f ≈ 300,000 km/s!

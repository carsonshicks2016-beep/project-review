1. Randomness to Order

Radioactive decay is fundamentally stochastic. A single atom of C-14 has a 50% chance of decaying in 5,730 years. It has no "memory" of how long it has existed. Yet, when you have billions of atoms, this pure quantum randomness averages out to produce an incredibly smooth, perfectly predictable exponential decay curve described by N(t) = N₀e^(-λt).

2. Radiocarbon Dating

Invented by Willard Libby in 1949, radiocarbon dating measures the ratio of unstable Carbon-14 to stable Carbon-12. Living things constantly exchange carbon with the atmosphere, maintaining a steady ratio. When they die, C-14 decays into Nitrogen-14. By measuring the remaining C-14, we can calculate the time elapsed since death using t = -t½ / ln(2) * ln(N/N₀).

3. Decay Chains & Equilibrium

Often, a radioactive "parent" nucleus decays into a "daughter" nucleus that is also radioactive. This forms a decay chain (e.g., Uranium-238 → Radium → Radon → Lead). Depending on the half-lives of the parent and daughter, they can reach Secular Equilibrium (where activities are equal) or Transient Equilibrium. The Bateman equations describe these complex overlapping dynamics.

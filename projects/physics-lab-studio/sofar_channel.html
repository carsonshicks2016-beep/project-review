1. Sound Speed Minimum

In the ocean, sound speed depends on temperature and pressure. Near the surface, the water is warm, so sound travels fast. As depth increases, temperature drops, causing sound speed to decrease. However, below about 1000m, the temperature becomes constant but the pressure continues to rise, causing sound speed to increase again. This creates a minimum sound speed at the SOFAR channel axis.

2. Ray Bending (Snell's Law)

Snell's law dictates that sound waves bend (refract) towards regions of lower sound speed. If a sound wave is emitted near the SOFAR axis and travels upward, the increasing sound speed bends it back down. If it travels downward, increasing pressure bends it back up. The sound becomes "trapped" in the channel, oscillating around the minimum speed axis without hitting the surface or seafloor.

3. Long Distance Travel

Because trapped sound rays do not lose energy to acoustic scattering at the rough sea surface or absorption in the muddy ocean floor, they can travel vast distances. Whales utilize this channel to communicate across entire ocean basins. During the Cold War, the US Navy deployed the SOSUS system to listen for submarines using the SOFAR channel.

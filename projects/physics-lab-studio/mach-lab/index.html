UNDER THE HOOD

Real propagation.
An idealized aircraft.

This is a physics-based auralization, not a recording or a prediction for a specific jet. A straight, level path passes directly above a stationary listener in uniform, still air at 343 m/s.

Arrival time sets the sound

t = τ + √((vτ)² + h²) / c

Each audio sample solves for emission time τ. Resampling the source at τ produces Doppler shift. Both causal roots are summed inside a supersonic cone. The reported pitch is the later-emission branch of a 200 Hz reference.

f / f₀ = 1 / |1 + v²τ / (cR)|
μ = asin(1 / M)
tboom = (h / c) √(1 − 1 / M²)

A finite shock, not an infinite spike

The boom begins at the geometric cone arrival. A configurable N-wave rises to +Δp, falls to −Δp, and returns to zero, with 2 ms rounded shock edges. Its pressure and duration are illustrative inputs; neither is inferred from Mach number alone.

What the model leaves out

The engine is a synthetic broadband source with harmonics, propagated with spherical spreading and a regularized retarded-time gain. The source spectrum is filtered for playback bandwidth. Speaker volume is uncalibrated. No real engine recording, aircraft shape, lift, atmospheric stratification, wind, absorption, terrain, ground reflection, or nonlinear propagation is solved. The displayed cone is the weak-disturbance Mach cone; real finite-strength shocks can differ, especially near Mach 1 and at hypersonic speed.

At exactly Mach 1 this point-source model has no finite-angle cone or added N-wave. Transonic local shocks need an aircraft flow model. The flight is assumed to have begun long before the visible window; the rings are illustrative wavefronts, not individual audible cycles.

# Civilia Brainstorm

## North Star

Build an emergent AI civilization sandbox: part simulation, part agent society, part strategy game, part weird little universe generator.

The goal is not just "agents gather wood." The good version is a living society where agents have personalities, memories, beliefs, goals, grudges, skills, secrets, social status, fears, habits, and odd fixations. They form families, factions, cults, guilds, governments, markets, rivalries, traditions, rumors, myths, art movements, and historical narratives.

The project should feel like watching a tiny world invent itself.

## Core Pillars

- Deterministic simulation for needs, resources, time, geography, production, travel, and events.
- Agent planning for goals, relationships, jobs, decisions, conflict, cooperation, and reputation.
- LLM-generated flavor for high-value moments: dialogue, rumors, laws, speeches, myths, books, trial records, newspapers, and biographies.
- Long-term memory for agents, factions, places, and civilizations.
- A world archive that records events, converts them into history, and later lets agents misremember or reinterpret them.
- A rich observer UI: map, timeline, social graph, economy, factions, agent profiles, memories, newspapers, and replay.

## MVP Shape

1. Tiny world with 30 agents.
2. Agents have needs, jobs, traits, relationships, and memories.
3. Time advances day by day.
4. Agents work, trade, talk, help, insult, gossip, and form opinions.
5. Events are stored in a timeline.
6. A daily or weekly chronicle summarizes emergent drama.
7. You can click an agent and read their inner life.
8. You can inspect a social graph and see alliances, rivalries, trust, romance, debts, mentorships, and grudges.

## Civilization Simulator Systems

### Individual Agents

- Personality traits: bold, anxious, vain, curious, stubborn, loyal, paranoid, generous, ambitious, impulsive, ritualistic, melancholic, theatrical.
- Needs: hunger, rest, safety, belonging, status, novelty, meaning, comfort.
- Private memories: personal events, witnessed events, conversations, betrayals, favors, debts, promises.
- Public reputation: brave, unreliable, wealthy, cursed, funny, dangerous, honorable, suspicious.
- Relationships: friends, rivals, mentors, apprentices, enemies, crushes, spouses, siblings, debtors, patrons, political allies.
- Skills: farming, mining, medicine, rhetoric, carpentry, smithing, poetry, law, espionage, engineering, teaching, cooking.
- Beliefs: true beliefs, false beliefs, rumors, superstitions, political views, religious ideas, historical interpretations.
- Goals: become rich, protect family, seek revenge, write poems, build a tower, expose corruption, found a school, start a cult, become mayor.
- Secrets: hidden crimes, forbidden romances, stolen goods, fake identities, private doubts, secret inventions.
- Habits and quirks: collects blue stones, hates soup, always challenges authority, writes anonymous pamphlets, talks to statues.

### Social Simulation

- Gossip spreads with distortion.
- Agents lie, exaggerate, omit, persuade, apologize, forgive, betray, and confess.
- Cliques form around jobs, beliefs, neighborhoods, age, wealth, or shared trauma.
- Reputation changes based on witnessed events and rumor networks.
- Social status influences elections, markets, marriage, jobs, trials, and faction recruitment.
- Agents can mentor each other and pass down skills, beliefs, and grudges.
- Families preserve myths and biased versions of history.
- Children inherit distorted stories from parents and teachers.
- Agents can coordinate: protests, strikes, festivals, mobs, rescues, building projects, conspiracies.

### Culture Engine

- Slang emerges from repeated events or famous people.
- Taboos form after disasters, betrayals, crimes, or religious interpretations.
- Fashion changes based on class, region, politics, rituals, and charismatic figures.
- Rituals and holidays emerge from historical events.
- Myths grow from misunderstood events.
- Cuisine evolves from available resources, trade, scarcity, and local pride.
- Architecture styles reflect materials, religion, wealth, climate, and political eras.
- Art movements form around trauma, rebellion, boredom, or elite patronage.
- Philosophical schools argue over made-up metaphysics.
- Local customs become identity markers.

Example: one town decides blue doors are unlucky because a famous betrayal happened behind one.

### Economy

- Farms, mines, workshops, markets, roads, warehouses, inns, docks, and trade routes.
- Wages, debt, prices, shortages, luxury goods, black markets, taxation, corruption.
- Production chains: grain to bread, ore to tools, wool to clothing, timber to buildings.
- Labor availability changes with war, plague, migration, politics, strikes, or fashion.
- Wealth inequality creates resentment, patronage, charity, crime, and revolt risk.
- Markets respond to shocks: drought, hoarding, crop disease, theft, new roads, tariffs.
- Guilds form around crafts and protect knowledge, wages, and status.
- Black markets emerge when goods are banned, scarce, or taxed too heavily.

### Politics

- Village councils, monarchies, merchant republics, theocracies, guild states, military juntas, anarchic communes.
- Elections, coups, councils, trials, protests, propaganda, bribery, intimidation.
- Laws are proposed, debated, passed, ignored, repealed, and mythologized.
- Factions have agendas, leaders, symbols, slogans, rivalries, and internal splits.
- Secret police, informants, resistance cells, banned books, underground rituals.
- Court trials with witnesses, evidence, lies, public opinion, and political pressure.
- A bureaucratic mistake can trigger famine, rebellion, or a legendary feud.
- Charismatic agents can accidentally become prophets, demagogues, reformers, or martyrs.

### History System

- The world keeps a timeline of events.
- Big events become named historical eras or incidents.
- Later agents reinterpret the past according to ideology, family story, faction loyalty, or bad memory.
- Historians, priests, teachers, politicians, and artists can rewrite history.
- Books, monuments, songs, festivals, and laws preserve biased memories.
- Agents can argue over what really happened.

Example event names:

- The Trial of Mara

- The Year of False Rain

- Rumors mutate as they spread.

## Signature Features

These are the features that make the project feel like more than a sim. They are the hooks: the things someone would remember after seeing it once.

### The Chronicle

A living newspaper/history generator that turns raw simulation events into dramatic records.

Core idea:

- Every day, week, season, or year, the world generates a chronicle from actual events.
- Different institutions produce different versions of the same truth: town paper, royal decree, rebel pamphlet, guild bulletin, religious sermon, private diary, schoolbook.
- Chronicles should be biased by whoever creates them.
- Important events get names, summaries, suspects, heroes, villains, and consequences.
- Old chronicles become historical sources that future agents can cite, misread, censor, or mythologize.

Possible article types:

- Scandal report
- War bulletin
- Market panic
- Obituary
- Festival announcement
- Trial coverage
- Political endorsement
- Rumor column
- Missing person notice
- Guild complaint
- Religious warning
- Scientific discovery
- Satirical poem

Cool details:

- Newspapers can be censored, forged, banned, or secretly distributed.
- Agents can become famous because of how they are portrayed.
- Public opinion shifts based on article framing.
- Competing newspapers can create factional realities.
- Later historians can compare conflicting sources.

UI:

- A readable newspaper view.
- Filters by settlement, faction, author, year, topic, and bias.
- Click any article to reveal the raw sim events behind it.
- "Show contested versions" button for multiple perspectives on the same incident.

### The Rumor River

A system for watching information mutate as it flows through society.

Core idea:

- Rumors start from witnessed events, lies, misunderstandings, dreams, propaganda, jokes, or accidents.
- Every retelling can mutate the rumor.
- Mutation depends on personality, relationship, fear, ideology, memory quality, social status, and incentives.
- A rumor can become gossip, panic, myth, propaganda, law, taboo, or war.

Mutation examples:

- A real theft becomes "the baker stole grain."
- Then "the baker hid grain for the mayor."
- Then "the mayor is starving the poor."
- Then "the old bridge is cursed because the stolen grain passed under it."
- Then a yearly bridge ritual begins.

Rumor attributes:

- Original event
- Current claim
- Truth distance
- Emotional charge
- Spread rate
- Known believers
- Known skeptics
- Source credibility
- Faction usefulness
- Mutation history

Agent behaviors:

- Gossip for bonding.
- Weaponize rumors against rivals.
- Suppress rumors to protect family.
- Investigate rumors.
- Publicly deny rumors.
- Pretend to believe rumors for political gain.
- Confess to stop worse rumors.

UI:

- A river/branching graph of rumor versions.
- Nodes are retellings; edges show who told whom.
- Color by truth distance, faction, or emotional tone.
- Click a rumor to see believers, skeptics, and consequences.
- "Replay spread" animation showing how a claim infected the town.

### The Artifact Ledger

Every meaningful object can accumulate provenance, symbolism, and myth.

Core idea:

- Objects are not just inventory items. Some become historical artifacts.
- An artifact remembers who owned it, where it went, what events it witnessed, and what people believe about it.
- The actual history and the believed history can diverge.
- Artifacts can become heirlooms, relics, trophies, cursed objects, legal evidence, political symbols, or museum pieces.

Artifact types:

- Weapons
- Tools
- Books
- Letters
- Clothing
- Jewelry
- Keys
- Banners
- Religious relics
- Legal documents
- Inventions
- Household objects
- Gifts
- Stolen goods

Ledger fields:

- True origin
- Claimed origin
- Owners
- Witnessed events
- Repairs and alterations
- Crimes connected to it
- Emotional associations
- Faction claims
- Monetary value
- Symbolic value
- Rumors attached
- Current location

Emergent stories:

- A plain kitchen knife becomes famous because it was used in a failed coup.
- A forged letter causes a war, then becomes a sacred document.
- A child's toy survives a disaster and becomes a town relic.
- A banner changes meaning after three different factions use it.
- A book is banned, copied, mistranslated, and eventually treated as prophecy.

UI:

- Artifact profile pages.
- Provenance timeline.
- Map of where the item has traveled.
- Difference between verified history and public myth.
- "Related people, events, factions, rumors" graph.

### The Culture Autopsy

A tool for explaining why a custom, ritual, taboo, law, fashion, phrase, holiday, or belief exists.

Core idea:

- Culture should not appear randomly. It should have ancestry.
- Any cultural element can be clicked to reveal the chain of events that produced it.
- The chain can include disasters, charismatic agents, economic changes, rumors, political choices, artistic trends, and accidents.

Examples:

- Blue doors are unlucky because a betrayal happened behind one, then a rumor spread, then a priest sermonized it, then builders avoided blue paint.
- A town bows before crossing bridges because a bridge collapse was blamed on arrogance.
- A slang word for "coward" comes from the name of a mayor who fled during a fire.
- A wedding ritual began as a tax dodge and became sacred tradition.
- A banned color becomes fashionable among rebels.

Autopsy structure:

- Cultural element
- First known appearance
- Trigger events
- Important amplifiers
- Agents who promoted it
- Agents who resisted it
- Institutional adoption
- Distortions over time
- Current believers and skeptics
- Practical effects on behavior

UI:

- Causal chain view.
- Timeline view.
- Social spread map.
- Confidence meter: what is verified versus guessed by in-world historians.
- Compare "true origin" with "popular origin story."

## Additional Bells and Whistles

### Agent Psychology Model

- Emotions: anger, fear, shame, pride, envy, grief, affection, boredom, awe, disgust.
- Long-term psychological states: trauma, obsession, loneliness, ambition, guilt, resentment, loyalty, insecurity.
- Coping patterns: withdrawal, overwork, revenge, art, religion, gossip, generosity, control.
- Personality should influence memory, risk tolerance, rumor mutation, career choice, relationship repair, and political behavior.
- Agents can develop fixations: prove a rival wrong, recover a lost object, avenge a parent, write the perfect song, expose a conspiracy.

### Religion and Myth Engine

- Agents interpret rare events as omens, miracles, curses, blessings, or punishments.
- Religions can form around founders, artifacts, disasters, celestial events, animals, mountains, ruins, dreams, or misunderstood science.
- Sects split over doctrine, leadership, rituals, calendars, food laws, burial practices, or sacred colors.
- Heresies can become mainstream if political conditions change.
- Saints, prophets, martyrs, relics, pilgrimages, temples, holy days, and forbidden texts.
- Different factions can weaponize theology for law, war, charity, hierarchy, or rebellion.

### Law, Crime, and Justice

- Crimes: theft, fraud, assault, smuggling, sabotage, slander, bribery, arson, treason, forgery, grave robbing, forbidden rituals.
- Justice systems: councils, courts, elders, kings, guild arbitration, religious judgment, mob justice.
- Trials include witnesses, evidence, memory reliability, public pressure, class bias, corruption, and precedent.
- Punishments: fines, exile, prison, forced labor, public apology, restitution, ritual cleansing, execution in darker worlds.
- Laws can produce loopholes, perverse incentives, black markets, protest, and legal professions.

### Language Drift

- Words shift meaning across generations, factions, and regions.
- Slang emerges from famous events, jokes, insults, jobs, and youth culture.
- Political slogans become common phrases.
- Sacred words become curses, or insults become badges of pride.
- Translations and copying errors can change law, religion, and history.
- Agents can misunderstand old texts because language has moved on.

### Fashion and Status Engine

- Clothing, colors, hair, jewelry, scars, uniforms, and accessories signal class, faction, job, religion, region, and rebellion.
- Fashion spreads through admiration, imitation, trade, celebrities, scarcity, and political identity.
- Sumptuary laws can restrict who may wear certain materials or colors.
- Counterculture styles become mainstream, then elite, then old-fashioned.
- A practical garment can become a sacred costume or faction uniform.

### Generational Memory

- Families pass down debts, grudges, heirlooms, recipes, stories, professions, alliances, and social rank.
- Children inherit biased versions of history.
- Old conflicts can outlive anyone who remembers the original reason.
- Family reputation affects marriage, credit, jobs, elections, trials, and trust.
- A descendant can reject, redeem, exploit, or be trapped by inherited identity.

### Secret Societies

- Hidden groups with initiation rituals, coded phrases, symbols, meeting places, and internal ranks.
- Possible goals: protect knowledge, overthrow rulers, preserve banned culture, enrich members, worship relics, control markets.
- Infiltration, paranoia, loyalty tests, double agents, purges, false accusations.
- Societies can be real, exaggerated, entirely fake, or invented by propaganda and then made real by believers.

### Architecture Evolution

- Towns physically change based on wealth, fear, religion, technology, disasters, climate, law, and social hierarchy.
- Walls appear after raids.
- Tall towers appear after status competition.
- Public squares shrink under authoritarian rule or expand under civic culture.
- Temples, guildhalls, prisons, markets, theaters, schools, and monuments reshape daily life.
- Ruins preserve memory and attract myths, squatters, treasure hunters, and reformers.

### Propaganda System

- Factions produce songs, posters, monuments, sermons, school lessons, plays, coins, flags, newspapers, and official histories.
- Propaganda can simplify events into heroes, villains, betrayals, and destiny.
- Agents vary in susceptibility based on trust, education, personality, trauma, and social pressure.
- Counter-propaganda can parody, debunk, or invert the message.
- Repeated propaganda can become "common sense" for future generations.

### Romance and Family Politics

- Romance can be affection, status strategy, rebellion, alliance-building, obsession, scandal, or tragedy.
- Marriages can merge wealth, settle feuds, create succession claims, or ignite resentment.
- Affairs create secrets, blackmail, illegitimate heirs, and factional crises.
- Inheritance disputes can alter politics and economics.
- Family elders can pressure younger agents into choices that create long-term drama.

### Disease and Medicine

- Diseases spread through travel, crowding, trade, weather, animals, water, and rituals.
- Medicine can include folk cures, skilled doctors, guild knowledge, religious healing, quarantine, and fraud.
- Scapegoats and conspiracy theories emerge during outbreaks.
- Public health laws can cause resistance, panic, smuggling, and heroism.
- Medical breakthroughs can shift power toward universities, guilds, temples, or the state.

### Exploration and Cultural Contact

- New lands, ruins, trade partners, migrants, envoys, refugees, colonists, and outsiders.
- Cultural clashes over food, dress, law, religion, property, honor, language, and timekeeping.
- Trade introduces new goods, diseases, ideas, weapons, crops, and status symbols.
- Explorers can lie, misunderstand, exaggerate, or become legends.
- Outsider myths can say more about the home culture than the outsiders.

### Meta-Simulation Tools

- Seed comparison: run 100 worlds and compare faction formation, inequality, violence, culture, and collapse.
- Timeline branching: rewind to an event and fork history.
- Counterfactuals: "what if the trial verdict changed?"
- Weirdness dial: from realistic village sim to surreal mythic fever dream.
- Culture autopsy search: find every tradition caused by famine, betrayal, weather, or propaganda.
- Emergence detector: automatically flag surprising cascades and rare social patterns.
- Historical heatmap: show where important events cluster over time.

## Realistic Mechanics Backlog

These are mechanics that create causal weirdness: small events turning into grudges, myths, laws, markets, reputations, rituals, and revolutions.

### Social Mechanics

- Favor Economy: agents track favors owed, favors given, unpaid debts, and social leverage.
- Social Exhaustion: charismatic or high-status agents can overextend and start losing trust.
- Public Face vs Private Self: agents can perform loyalty while privately doubting, resenting, or plotting.
- Embarrassment Memory: small humiliations can create lifelong rivalries or avoidance patterns.
- Patronage Chains: powerful agents protect weaker ones in exchange for loyalty, labor, votes, silence, or prestige.
- Reputation Lag: people keep believing old reputations after behavior changes.
- Scapegoat Cycle: during crisis, societies search for someone to blame.
- Clique Gravity: agents slowly become more like the groups they spend time with.
- Moral Contagion: one dramatic event shifts what the town considers acceptable or unforgivable.
- Forgiveness Thresholds: apologies only work if timing, status, relationship, sincerity, restitution, and witnesses align.
- Social Proof: agents become more likely to accept behavior, fashion, belief, or panic when many peers already do.
- Status Contamination: association with a disgraced person, family, job, or faction lowers reputation.
- Honor Accounting: insults, public slights, and unavenged harms accumulate social pressure.
- Gossip Reciprocity: agents share gossip partly to bond, but repeated gossiping can reduce trust.
- Face-Saving Rituals: societies invent polite procedures for surrender, apology, defeat, debt, and rejection.
- Bridge Figures: agents with ties across factions can prevent conflict or become targets for both sides.

### Belief and Knowledge Mechanics

- Epistemic Weather: eras can be paranoid, optimistic, skeptical, mystical, legalistic, conspiratorial, or reformist.
- Source Trust Graph: agents trust different people, institutions, books, newspapers, families, guilds, temples, or factions.
- Official Truth vs Street Truth: legal records and public belief can diverge.
- Forbidden Knowledge: banned ideas become more prestigious underground.
- Proof Objects: artifacts, letters, scars, ruins, ledgers, maps, and witnesses function as evidence.
- Misremembered Witnesses: eyewitnesses can confidently distort events.
- Belief Inertia: agents resist facts that threaten identity, status, livelihood, family loyalty, or faction membership.
- Conspiracy Formation: unrelated events get stitched into a false pattern.
- Expert Class: scholars, priests, doctors, judges, engineers, and elders compete over authority.
- Historical Revision Waves: new regimes rewrite the meaning of old events.
- Rumor Immunity: agents become harder to persuade after being fooled, betrayed, or publicly embarrassed.
- Confirmation Hunger: agents seek sources that make existing fears or hopes feel justified.
- Information Bottlenecks: remote towns, illiteracy, censorship, weather, travel cost, and language barriers slow knowledge.
- Tacit Knowledge: some skills cannot be learned from books and require apprenticeship, practice, or guild access.
- Credibility Cascades: once enough prestigious sources repeat a claim, disbelief becomes socially expensive.
- Archives as Power: whoever controls records, maps, birth registers, tax ledgers, and legal documents controls reality.

### Culture Mechanics

- Prestige Drift: low-status customs can become elite fashion, then stale tradition.
- Taboo Birth: an event plus fear plus repetition creates a taboo.
- Ritual Mutation: rituals lose, gain, simplify, or invert steps over generations.
- Symbol Capture: a faction adopts a color, object, song, animal, phrase, or gesture and changes its meaning.
- Cultural Fossils: customs survive after their original purpose is forgotten.
- Regional Dialects: towns develop slang, accents, insults, idioms, nicknames, and politeness rules.
- Taste Makers: artists, nobles, rebels, saints, athletes, generals, or scandalous lovers make behaviors fashionable.
- Meme Wars: slogans, songs, jokes, rumors, insults, and symbols compete for adoption.
- Sacred Geography: places become holy, cursed, prestigious, shameful, or politically loaded.
- Festival Politics: public holidays become arenas for propaganda, protest, courtship, commerce, and status display.
- Generational Rebellion: young agents reject older customs, then later become defenders of their own era's norms.
- Cultural Borrowing: customs imported from outsiders are adapted, misunderstood, mocked, banned, or made prestigious.
- Etiquette Traps: agents can gain or lose status by knowing obscure rituals, greetings, clothing rules, and taboos.
- Moral Fashion: compassion, austerity, patriotism, piety, toughness, refinement, or rebellion can become socially fashionable.
- Local Exceptionalism: towns create stories about why they are uniquely brave, cursed, pure, clever, or chosen.
- Folk Explanations: practical rules gain symbolic explanations after the original reason disappears.

### Politics and Law Mechanics

- Precedent Engine: court decisions shape future cases.
- Loophole Discovery: agents exploit poorly written laws.
- Bureaucratic Drift: institutions gradually serve their own survival.
- Office Corruption: power changes agents slowly through favors, access, flattery, fear, and isolation.
- Coalition Bargaining: factions trade favors, laws, marriages, pardons, appointments, contracts, and silence.
- Legitimacy Meter: rulers need more than force; they need belief, ritual, competence, tradition, fear, or prosperity.
- Crisis Powers: emergency laws solve problems while creating future authoritarian residue.
- Succession Anxiety: unclear inheritance creates paranoia before anyone dies.
- Public Trial Theater: trials become symbolic events, not just justice.
- Soft Coups: factions capture institutions without open revolt.
- Administrative Distance: laws get distorted as they move from capital to town to clerk to guard.
- Selective Enforcement: the same law hits different classes, factions, families, and outsiders differently.
- Legal Memory: old rulings, ancient charters, and forgotten promises can be revived as weapons.
- Regulatory Capture: guilds, merchants, temples, or nobles shape rules to protect themselves.
- Petition Culture: ordinary agents learn which complaints, phrases, witnesses, and rituals make authorities respond.
- Mandate Narratives: rulers justify power through divine favor, prosperity, ancestry, security, reform, or revenge.

### Economy Mechanics

- Trust-Based Credit: loans depend on family, reputation, caste or class, rumor, collateral, and patronage.
- Panic Buying: fear creates shortages even when supply is fine.
- Luxury Signaling: goods matter because of what they say socially.
- Guild Secrets: craftspeople hide techniques to preserve power.
- Counterfeit Goods: fake relics, bad medicine, forged documents, imitation luxury, watered wine, debased coin.
- Moral Markets: some goods become shameful, sacred, patriotic, foreign, polluting, or forbidden.
- Inheritance Locks: old wealth keeps shaping politics generations later.
- Labor Identity: jobs become cultures with pride, songs, grudges, rituals, jokes, and politics.
- Trade Route Mythology: distant goods bring rumors and imagined foreignness.
- Economic Martyrs: fired workers, ruined farmers, cheated merchants, and evicted tenants become symbols.
- Price Memory: agents remember old prices and treat changes as injustice even when conditions shift.
- Hoarding Spiral: expectations of scarcity create real scarcity.
- Middleman Suspicion: brokers, tax collectors, moneylenders, and merchants become targets during crisis.
- Patron Markets: buying from the right seller can signal loyalty, faction identity, religion, or class.
- Seasonal Labor Rhythms: planting, harvest, festivals, winter, storms, and migration alter work and politics.
- Debt Inheritance: financial obligations pass through families, marriages, guilds, and patronage chains.

### Weird High-Value Mechanics

- The One Bad Winter: a single crisis permanently rewires culture, law, diet, architecture, politics, and family stories.
- Accidental Prophet: someone's wrong prediction becomes true-looking by coincidence.
- Institutional Amnesia: the town follows a rule nobody understands anymore.
- Mythic Feedback Loop: people act on a myth, making the myth socially real.
- Cursed Object by Consensus: an artifact has no magic, but belief makes everyone behave around it as if it does.
- Enemy Image Engine: factions invent simplified villains and then organize around opposing them.
- Children's Version of History: what schools teach becomes future politics.
- The Archive Lies: records can be incomplete, biased, forged, mistranslated, censored, or destroyed.
- Aesthetic Revolutions: art style changes become political alignment.
- Cultural Immune System: societies reject ideas that threaten their identity, even useful ones.
- Panic Architecture: one disaster causes generations of overbuilt walls, gates, towers, storehouses, or flood channels.
- Martyr Inflation: factions compete to claim victims as symbols, weakening or intensifying the original event.
- Reform Backlash: a successful reform creates new losers who preserve resentment for decades.
- Ritualized Inefficiency: communities preserve inefficient practices because they signal identity or morality.
- Suspicion of Convenience: agents distrust solutions that feel too easy, foreign, or humiliating.
- Prophecy Retrofitting: old vague statements are reinterpreted after events make them seem meaningful.

### Demographics and Family Structure

- Birth Rates: security, food, wealth, religion, law, disease, and war influence family size.
- Marriage Markets: families consider love, wealth, reputation, alliances, fertility, inheritance, and scandal.
- Household Economics: households pool labor, food, childcare, elder care, secrets, and debt.
- Age Cohorts: generations share formative crises and develop distinct politics, aesthetics, and anxieties.
- Elder Authority: older agents control memory, inheritance, ritual knowledge, and social permission.
- Youth Bulges: many young adults can increase rebellion, migration, innovation, unemployment, and fashion churn.
- Orphans and Wards: disasters create dependents whose loyalties and resentments shape future politics.
- Adoption and Fostering: families use care networks to form alliances, repay debts, or conceal scandal.
- Lineage Prestige: ancestry can open doors even when current ability is weak.
- Family Fragmentation: migration, exile, debt, feud, disease, and marriage split family networks across regions.

### Institutions and Organizations

- Institutional Memory: organizations preserve procedures after founders die.
- Mission Drift: guilds, temples, councils, and schools slowly shift purpose.
- Internal Factions: organizations contain competing wings, reformers, hardliners, careerists, and idealists.
- Membership Gatekeeping: groups control entry through exams, lineage, bribes, rituals, sponsorship, or ideology.
- Professional Ethics: jobs develop codes of conduct that can conflict with law, family, or religion.
- Whistleblowing: insiders reveal corruption, incompetence, heresy, or dangerous secrets.
- Institutional Rivalry: temples, courts, guilds, newspapers, schools, and militias compete for authority.
- Resource Dependence: institutions become loyal to whoever funds, feeds, protects, or legitimizes them.
- Public Trust Cycles: scandals weaken institutions; competence during crisis restores them.
- Succession Rules: leadership transitions create splits, purges, reforms, and legitimacy disputes.

### Environment and Infrastructure

- Water Politics: wells, rivers, canals, mills, bridges, and irrigation become sources of power and conflict.
- Seasonal Risk: flood, drought, frost, heat, storms, pests, and disease change yearly priorities.
- Food Storage: granaries, spoilage, rationing, theft, and hoarding determine crisis survival.
- Road Quality: travel time, trade, rumor spread, migration, and military power depend on infrastructure.
- Building Materials: timber, stone, clay, metal, and thatch shape architecture, fire risk, status, and trade.
- Fire Ecology: dense towns face fire risk; disasters reshape law, insurance, architecture, and blame.
- Waste and Sanitation: crowding produces smell, disease, class stigma, labor roles, and public works politics.
- Animal Labor: horses, oxen, dogs, and livestock affect wealth, transport, diet, disease, and status.
- Resource Depletion: overfarming, deforestation, overfishing, and mining change economy and migration.
- Borderlands: frontiers, roads, mountains, rivers, and forests create smuggling, myth, refuge, and cultural mixing.

### Conflict, Violence, and Security

- Feud Logic: retaliations follow social rules and can escalate or be contained by mediators.
- Militia Mobilization: defense depends on trust, training, equipment, leadership, and harvest timing.
- Banditry and Protection: insecurity creates protection markets, strongmen, patrols, bribes, and fear.
- Weapon Prestige: carrying certain weapons signals rank, job, faction, masculinity, rebellion, or threat.
- Trauma After Violence: survivors alter beliefs, architecture, rituals, politics, and child-rearing.
- Peace Rituals: societies need ceremonies, exchanges, marriages, hostage arrangements, or trials to end conflict.
- Security Theater: visible guards, gates, inspections, and oaths may reassure people more than they reduce risk.
- Collective Punishment: authorities punish families, neighborhoods, or guilds, creating long resentment.
- War Economy: conflict shifts labor, prices, status, technology, propaganda, medicine, and taxation.
- Veteran Networks: former fighters carry skills, trauma, status, secrets, and political influence.

### Education and Socialization

- Apprenticeship: skills, values, secrets, and grudges pass through work relationships.
- School Curriculum: what children learn shapes future politics and social norms.
- Hidden Curriculum: schools teach obedience, rivalry, class markers, gender norms, ritual timing, and prestige habits.
- Literacy Divide: literate agents access contracts, law, scripture, propaganda, and long-distance power.
- Teacher Influence: beloved or hated teachers can shape a generation's beliefs.
- Coming-of-Age Rituals: societies mark adulthood with tests, ceremonies, work, service, or public humiliation.
- Peer Culture: children create their own slang, games, rankings, dares, and myths.
- Punishment Memory: harsh discipline can produce obedience, resentment, reform movements, or secret solidarity.
- Knowledge Monopolies: guilds, temples, families, and schools restrict who may learn valuable skills.
- Educational Scandals: cheating, favoritism, forbidden books, or abused authority become political events.

### Health, Bodies, and Care

- Disability and Accommodation: injury, age, illness, and impairment shape work, status, family roles, and architecture.
- Care Burden: illness changes household labor, debt, gender roles, inheritance, and resentment.
- Folk Diagnosis: communities explain illness through weather, morality, spirits, food, class, outsiders, or labor.
- Medical Reputation: healers gain or lose trust through outcomes, bedside manner, price, and faction ties.
- Public Health Tradeoffs: quarantine, sanitation, burial rules, and travel restrictions create moral conflict.
- Body Ideals: cultures develop norms for beauty, strength, scars, age, modesty, and visible labor.
- Nutrition Effects: diet influences health, fertility, work capacity, class identity, and ritual purity.
- Grief Practices: mourning rituals shape memory, inheritance, debt, social repair, and political symbolism.
- Epidemic Memory: disease outbreaks produce lasting taboos, saints, scapegoats, laws, and architecture.
- Care Professions: midwives, nurses, gravediggers, healers, cooks, and cleaners develop status and secrets.

### Migration, Outsiders, and Identity

- Refugee Waves: disasters, wars, famine, and persecution move people with skills, trauma, and stories.
- Assimilation Pressure: newcomers adopt local language, clothing, names, rituals, and politics unevenly.
- Diaspora Networks: migrants maintain trade, marriage, religious, and information ties across regions.
- Outsider Scapegoating: strangers become easy blame targets during crisis.
- Cultural Hybrids: mixed families, border towns, and trade hubs create new customs.
- Identity Passing: agents conceal origin, class, religion, family, or faction ties to survive.
- Return Migration: agents who leave and come back bring new ideas, wealth, shame, or suspicion.
- Guest Rights: hospitality norms regulate strangers, travelers, envoys, merchants, and refugees.
- Boundary Maintenance: food rules, clothing rules, accents, names, and rituals mark who belongs.
- Cosmopolitan Backlash: openness creates prosperity and identity anxiety at the same time.

### Media and Communication

- Message Degradation: letters are delayed, damaged, mistranslated, intercepted, forged, or read aloud badly.
- Public Reading: literate agents become information brokers for illiterate crowds.
- Censorship Workarounds: jokes, songs, symbols, clothing, and coded language carry forbidden messages.
- Pamphlet Storms: cheap media accelerates factional conflict and rumor spread.
- Performance Politics: plays, sermons, speeches, and festivals shape belief through emotion.
- Witness Networks: who was physically present at an event matters.
- Silence as Signal: refusal to comment can be interpreted as guilt, loyalty, fear, wisdom, or contempt.
- Seal and Signature Trust: documents depend on marks, messengers, witnesses, seals, and known handwriting.
- News Lag: distant events arrive late, creating mismatched decisions and preventable conflict.
- Communication Rituals: formal greetings, titles, petitions, and apologies affect whether messages are accepted.

### Personal Life and Daily Texture

- Routine Comfort: agents prefer familiar routes, meals, work rhythms, and social spaces.
- Domestic Conflict: chores, money, in-laws, children, secrets, and status create household drama.
- Leisure Culture: games, songs, sport, gambling, drinking, storytelling, theater, and festivals build identity.
- Food Preference: tastes are shaped by class, scarcity, religion, memory, season, and childhood.
- Private Hobbies: art, collecting, gardening, invention, reading, gambling, exercise, and superstition.
- Sleep and Fatigue: tired agents make worse decisions, remember poorly, and become irritable.
- Mood Spillover: personal problems affect work, politics, gossip, parenting, and belief.
- Daily Routes: repeated paths create chance meetings, local familiarity, territoriality, and witness opportunities.
- Personal Style: agents express identity through tools, clothing, posture, speech, home decor, and habits.
- Minor Annoyance Accumulation: repeated small frictions can become major social breaks.

## Technical Architecture Sketch

- Core simulation loop: deterministic and inspectable.
- World state database: agents, places, factions, resources, events, memories, relationships.
- Event bus: every meaningful action emits structured events.
- Memory system: agents store compressed memories with emotional weight, source reliability, decay, and tags.
- Belief system: separates observed facts, rumors, interpretations, ideology, and private suspicion.
- Planner: agents choose actions from needs, goals, opportunities, personality, relationships, and beliefs.
- LLM layer: used sparingly for flavor, not core truth. Generate dialogue, speeches, newspapers, myths, books, laws, biographies.
- Embeddings/vector search: retrieve relevant memories, past events, cultural motifs, and personal context.
- UI: map, timeline, agent cards, social graph, faction browser, economy panel, chronicle reader, replay mode.
- Experiment runner: run many seeds, compare outcomes, detect interesting histories.

## Local LLM and Intelligence Stack

Civilia should use a local Ollama stack for intelligence-heavy moments, but the simulation itself must remain structured, deterministic, and inspectable.

The LLM should not be the whole brain of every agent. It should be a co-processor used for language, interpretation, reflection, narrative, and occasional planning. The source of truth remains the world state and event log.

### Target Hardware

Current development target:

- Machine: MacBook Pro
- Chip: Apple M2 Pro
- CPU cores: 12 total, 8 performance and 4 efficiency
- Memory: 16 GB unified memory
- Architecture: arm64
- Practical implication: use small and medium local models, avoid giant models in the live loop, and batch/defer expensive generation.

### Model Roles

#### qwen3:4b - Citizen Workhorse

Use as the main local text model for frequent, lightweight intelligence tasks.

Why:

- Small enough for the 16 GB Mac target.
- Ollama lists `qwen3:4b` at about 2.5 GB with a 256K context window.
- Good fit for short reasoning, summarization, roleplay, small decisions, and structured JSON output.

Use cases:

- Agent reflection after meaningful events.
- Memory compression: turn raw events into compact personal memories.
- Belief update summaries: explain how an agent interpreted a witnessed event.
- Rumor mutation: generate one distorted retelling from one speaker to one listener.
- Short dialogue snippets between agents.
- Apologies, insults, excuses, pleas, threats, jokes, and small talk.
- Personal letters, diary fragments, prayers, petitions, and gossip notes.
- Naming small customs, nicknames, local insults, and inside jokes.
- Generating candidate goals for an agent from current pressures.
- Producing a short "inner thought" for the UI when the player clicks an agent.
- Rewriting structured facts into character-colored language.
- Explaining why an agent chose an action, based only on provided state.

Example prompt contract:

```text
You are not allowed to invent world facts.
Use only the supplied agent state, memories, relationships, beliefs, and event context.
Return JSON matching the requested schema.
If the agent would be uncertain, represent uncertainty rather than resolving it.
```

Recommended output types:

- `AgentReflection`
- `MemorySummary`
- `RumorVariant`
- `DialogueLine`
- `BeliefUpdate`
- `GoalCandidate`
- `PrivateThought`

#### qwen3:8b - Chronicle and History Director

Use as the higher-quality local model for less frequent, high-impact narrative and cultural tasks.

Why:

- Ollama lists `qwen3:8b` at about 5.2 GB with a 40K context window.
- Stronger than the 4B model while still reasonable on the target Mac if called selectively.
- Better for synthesizing many events into coherent stories, institutions, laws, and cultural shifts.

Use cases:

- The Chronicle: weekly, seasonal, yearly, or crisis newspapers.
- Major speeches, sermons, manifestos, trial summaries, royal decrees, rebel pamphlets.
- Culture Autopsy summaries.
- Myth formation after disasters, coincidences, wars, famines, or strange artifacts.
- Religion and sect descriptions.
- Political ideology summaries.
- Faction platforms and propaganda.
- Court opinions and legal precedent summaries.
- Historical era naming.
- Obituaries for important agents.
- Multi-perspective summaries of contested events.
- "What does this event mean to each faction?" synthesis.
- Long-form biographies for important agents.
- Schoolbook versions of history.
- In-world historian analysis.

Example prompt contract:

```text
You are the narrative synthesis layer for Civilia.
The event log is authoritative.
You may add style, bias, rhetoric, metaphor, and interpretation.
You may not add new events unless explicitly asked to propose possibilities.
Separate verified facts, public claims, faction framing, and uncertain interpretation.
```

Recommended output types:

- `ChronicleIssue`
- `FactionPamphlet`
- `TrialRecord`
- `LawDraft`
- `MythText`
- `CultureAutopsyReport`
- `HistoricalEraSummary`
- `PublicOpinionBrief`

Use for embeddings and search, not generation.

Why:

- Ollama lists `nomic-embed-text` at about 274 MB with a 2K context window.

Use cases:

### Invocation Rules

The normal sim tick should not call the LLM.

Normal tick:

```text
advance time
update needs
retrieve local memories
score possible actions
choose action with utility AI + personality + randomness
validate action against world rules
emit structured event
update world state
```

LLM-assisted tick:

```text
interesting event detected
gather relevant structured context
retrieve memories with embeddings
call qwen3:4b or qwen3:8b
validate returned JSON/schema
convert output into structured event, memory, belief, text artifact, or UI text
record provenance: model, prompt type, source events, timestamp
```

Call `qwen3:4b` when:

- One agent needs a reflection.
- One rumor mutates.
- Two agents need a short dialogue exchange.
- A memory needs compression.
- A small belief update needs natural-language explanation.
- A local custom, nickname, insult, or phrase needs flavor.

Call `qwen3:8b` when:

- Many events need synthesis.
- The output becomes public history.
- A faction, institution, religion, law, or culture is being shaped.
- The player is reading a Chronicle issue.
- A major trial, revolt, scandal, famine, war, schism, or succession crisis happens.

Call `nomic-embed-text` when:

- Storing a memory, event, article, law, rumor, artifact entry, or cultural element.
- Retrieving relevant past context before a model call.
- Searching the archive, memory system, rumor graph, or artifact ledger.

Do not call an LLM for:

- Basic hunger, sleep, work, and movement decisions.
- Every agent every tick.
- Determining whether an action is physically or legally possible.
- Updating resources, prices, locations, kinship, inventories, or event truth.
- Anything requiring canonical world-state mutation without validation.

### Agent "Free Will" Model

Agents feel autonomous because they have private state, limited knowledge, conflicting motives, and real consequences.

Each agent decision should combine:

- Needs: hunger, safety, rest, status, belonging, novelty, meaning.
- Personality: risk tolerance, pride, empathy, paranoia, ambition, curiosity, loyalty.
- Emotions: anger, fear, shame, grief, envy, affection, boredom, awe.
- Goals: immediate, long-term, social, economic, ideological, romantic, revenge-driven.
- Relationships: trust, debt, kinship, rivalry, patronage, attraction, suspicion.
- Memories: direct experiences, compressed summaries, emotional scars, promises, humiliations.
- Beliefs: facts, rumors, interpretations, ideology, superstition, uncertainty.
- Constraints: location, time, law, resources, class, faction, witnesses, skills.

Action selection should use utility scoring plus weighted randomness:

```text
candidate actions -> score each action -> apply personality modifiers -> apply social risk -> apply randomness -> choose valid action
```

Example:

```text
Steal bread:
  + hunger relief
  + revenge if angry at baker
  - fear of punishment
  - moral penalty
  - witness risk
  - reputation risk

Ask cousin for food:
  + hunger relief
  + kinship trust
  - pride cost
  - favor debt
```

The LLM may explain, phrase, or reflect on the choice, but the structured system chooses and validates the action.

### Prompt Context Budgeting

Never dump the whole world into a prompt.

For `qwen3:4b`, provide:

- Agent profile summary.
- Current location and situation.
- Top 5 to 20 relevant memories.
- Current relationships relevant to the scene.
- Current beliefs relevant to the scene.
- The event or decision being interpreted.
- The exact output schema.

For `qwen3:8b`, provide:

- A curated event packet.
- Relevant faction summaries.
- Public opinion state.
- Important rumors and contradictions.
- Relevant laws, artifacts, and cultural context.
- Desired publication type and bias.
- The exact output schema.

### Performance Strategy

- Keep `nomic-embed-text` available for retrieval.
- Use `qwen3:4b` for frequent small tasks.
- Use `qwen3:8b` sparingly for high-value synthesis.
- Queue LLM jobs asynchronously so the simulation can keep running.
- Cache outputs by prompt hash and source event IDs.
- Generate long chronicles in the background.
- Let the UI show placeholders like "the archive is compiling this account" if needed.
- Track token counts, latency, failure rate, and model used for every generation.
- Allow an "LLM budget per day" setting so large simulations do not melt the laptop.

### Reliability and Validation

- Validate every JSON response.
- Reject outputs that invent unsupported world facts.
- Store model-generated text separately from canonical world facts.
- Keep provenance: source event IDs, prompt type, model name, generation time.
- Allow deterministic fallbacks when the model fails.
- Use small retry prompts that ask only to repair JSON, not regenerate content.
- Treat model text as interpretation unless converted into a validated structured event.
- Never let the model directly mutate core world state.

## Civilia Implementation Blueprint

This section translates the brainstorm into a buildable program. The goal is to make the first version small enough to finish, but architected so the strange, realistic systems can keep accreting over time.

### Implementation Philosophy

- Build a deterministic simulation first, then add language and narrative.
- Treat the event log as the spine of the world.
- Treat model-generated text as interpretation, not truth.
- Keep the first town small enough to inspect by hand.
- Add only systems that emit structured events.
- Make every surprising outcome explainable through event history, memories, beliefs, and decision scores.
- Prefer boring data structures over clever abstractions until the simulation proves what it needs.
- Build debug tools early, because emergent systems become impossible to reason about without x-ray vision.

### MVP Boundary

The first playable/simulatable version should be a single-town social simulator.

MVP includes:

- One town.
- 20 to 50 agents.
- Daily ticks with optional hour-level substeps later.
- Locations: homes, market, well, bakery, farm, workshop, council hall, tavern, shrine, road.
- Agent needs: hunger, rest, safety, money, belonging, status.
- Agent traits: ambition, empathy, pride, paranoia, curiosity, risk tolerance, sociability.
- Jobs: farmer, baker, smith, trader, priest, clerk, guard, healer, laborer, unemployed.
- Relationships: trust, affection, resentment, debt, kinship, rivalry, patronage.
- Memories: witnessed events, conversations, favors, insults, thefts, promises.
- Beliefs: facts, rumors, suspicions, superstitions, public claims.
- Actions: work, eat, sleep, travel, talk, gossip, trade, help, insult, steal, accuse, apologize, investigate, attend meeting, write note.
- Event log: every meaningful action emits a typed event.
- Rumor River: rumors spread and mutate.
- Chronicle: periodic town newspaper/summary.
- Agent profile UI.
- Event timeline UI.
- Basic social graph UI.
- Debug inspector for agent decisions.

MVP does not include yet:

- War.
- Multi-town diplomacy.
- Dynasties and succession.

- Training/RL.

### First Playable Scenario

The first scenario should be intentionally small and drama-rich.

Working scenario:

- Town: Ashvale.
- Population: 32 agents.
- Economy: grain, bread, tools, wages, rent, small savings.
- Factions: council loyalists, market guild, shrine circle, labor families.
- Starting tension: poor harvest and rising bread prices.
- Starting secrets:
  - The baker has a private grain reserve.
  - A council clerk altered a tax ledger.
  - A guard owes money to a trader.
  - A young poet writes anonymous political verses.
  - Two families remember different versions of an old bridge accident.
- First expected emergent arc: hunger -> price resentment -> gossip -> accusation -> faction framing -> Chronicle coverage -> public pressure.

Why this is good:

- It exercises economy, reputation, rumor, law, class, public truth, private truth, and the Chronicle without needing a huge world.
- It gives the simulation enough pressure to produce events.
- It creates obvious debug cases: who believes the baker is hoarding, why, and what they do about it.

### Core Runtime Loop

The simulation should advance in deterministic ticks.

Basic daily loop:

```text
start day
  update weather/season/context
  update agent needs
  update location availability
  generate daily obligations
  for each phase of day:
    select active agents
    for each active agent:
      build perception packet
      retrieve relevant memories
      generate candidate actions
      score candidate actions
      choose one valid action with seeded randomness
      apply action effects
      emit structured events
      enqueue reactions
    process reaction systems
    process rumor spread
    update relationships/reputations/beliefs
  generate end-of-day summaries
  enqueue LLM jobs for notable moments
  persist snapshot and events
end day
```

Initial phases:

- Dawn: wake, hunger/rest update, household interactions.
- Morning: work, travel, market setup.
- Midday: trade, gossip, meals, public interactions.
- Afternoon: work continuation, errands, investigations.
- Evening: tavern, shrine, family, council, gossip.
- Night: sleep, theft, secret meetings, private reflection.

### Determinism and Randomness

Civilia should use seeded randomness for reproducibility.

Rules:

- Every world has a `world_seed`.
- Each simulation tick derives deterministic sub-seeds for agents and systems.
- Random choices should be logged when they change meaningful outcomes.
- LLM text is not deterministic by default, so model outputs need provenance and cache keys.
- Core simulation should still replay if LLM text is unavailable, using stored generated outputs or deterministic fallback strings.

This makes it possible to:

- Replay history.
- Branch from a day.
- Compare alternate timelines.
- Debug why something happened.
- Run experiments across seeds.

### Canonical State vs Derived Interpretation

Civilia needs a strict separation:

- Canonical state: structured facts the simulation accepts as true.
- Agent beliefs: what a specific agent thinks may be true.
- Public claims: what is being said publicly.
- Generated prose: readable interpretation of structured facts.
- Debug analysis: out-of-world explanation for the developer/player.

Example:

```text
Canonical state:
  The baker owns 18 grain units in cellar_02.

Agent belief:
  Mara believes the baker may be hiding grain for the council.

Public claim:
  "The baker and mayor are starving us."

Generated prose:
  The Chronicle says the market has become "a theater of hungry accusations."
```

The model may write prose and suggest interpretations. It may not silently change canonical state.

### Agent Decision Formula

Agents should not make perfect choices. They should make motivated, bounded, socially biased choices.

Candidate score:

```text
score(action) =
  need_value
  + goal_value
  + relationship_value
  + emotion_value
  + personality_value
  + belief_value
  + habit_value
  + social_pressure
  + opportunity_value
  - resource_cost
  - time_cost
  - risk_cost
  - moral_cost
  - reputation_cost
```

Then:

```text
final_score = score + seeded_noise + mood_noise
```

Selection:

- Use weighted random selection among plausible actions.
- Do not always pick the top action.
- Filter out actions that violate hard constraints.
- Allow rare impulsive actions when emotion is high.
- Allow habitual actions to win when nothing urgent is happening.

Decision trace should store:

- Candidate action IDs.
- Raw scores.
- Top reasons for each score.
- Memories retrieved.
- Beliefs considered.
- Relationships considered.
- Random seed/noise value.
- Final selected action.

### Risk List

Big risks:

- Too many mechanics before the core loop works.
- Calling the LLM too often.
- Model outputs inventing world facts.
- Events becoming inconsistent or underspecified.
- Agent decisions feeling random instead of motivated.
- UI becoming unreadable because everything is connected to everything.
- No replay/debug tooling, making bugs impossible to trace.
- Prematurely building multi-town civilization before one town is alive.

Mitigations:

- Keep MVP small.
- Make every system event-driven.
- Store decision traces.
- Add debug UI before polish.
- Validate model outputs.
- Keep canonical truth separate from generated prose.
- Use milestones as gates.

## Civilia Development Tools

- Scenario Editor: create settlements, factions, resources, laws, cultural seeds, artifacts, disasters, and starting secrets.
- Experiment Seed Explorer: replay alternate histories from the same starting world.
- Timeline Brancher: rewind to any event, fork the timeline, and compare what changes.
- Agent Memory Inspector: inspect what an agent knows, believes, doubts, and misremembers.
- Belief Map: compare what each agent, family, faction, town, newspaper, and religion thinks is true.
- Rumor Search Engine: find every claim connected to an event, agent, artifact, law, or faction.
- Culture Autopsy Search: find every custom caused by famine, betrayal, weather, propaganda, migration, or crime.
- Artifact Browser: search artifacts by owner, location, event, myth, faction claim, and symbolic value.
- Chronicle Archive: browse newspapers, decrees, sermons, diaries, pamphlets, trial records, and schoolbook versions of history.
- Emergence Detector: flag surprising social cascades, new rituals, rare disasters, strange alliances, and faction splits.
- Historical Heatmap: show where important events cluster over time.
- Social Graph Explorer: inspect trust, kinship, romance, debt, rivalry, mentorship, authority, and faction ties.
- Economy Inspector: watch prices, shortages, wealth inequality, trade routes, debt, wages, hoarding, and black markets.
- Law and Trial Viewer: inspect laws, court records, precedents, witness reliability, verdicts, punishments, and public reaction.
- Weirdness Dial: tune the world from realistic village sim to surreal mythic fever dream.

## Focused Direction

Civilia should become a living laboratory for agents, cultures, memory, politics, economy, narrative, and emergent weirdness.

Start with the core civilization engine:

- Agents with memory, needs, relationships, beliefs, and goals.
- A deterministic event-based world simulation.
- A culture, politics, economy, law, religion, and family layer.
- The Chronicle, Rumor River, Artifact Ledger, and Culture Autopsy as signature features.
- A visual UI for watching, inspecting, replaying, and branching everything.

Then expand within the Civilia world:

- Add more personality depth.
- Add procedural cultures and political systems.
- Add counterfactual timelines.
- Add experiment tools to compare seeds and civilizations.
- Add a weirdness engine that injects novel artifacts, myths, laws, disasters, social rules, and historical misunderstandings.

The project should stay focused on one thing: making a tiny civilization feel endlessly alive.
